Statutory supplements, built to order

No one should pay $250 for public law.

Pick the statutes and rules your course uses, from Securities Act § 5 to Rule 10b-5 and Regulation D, and download your own book-quality supplement, built from the official U.S. Code and eCFR. It is free, and there are no accounts.

§ 1How it works

Three steps, and no account.

  1. 1

    Choose provisions.

    Search by citation, or add a whole act, chapter, or regulation in one click.

    • § 515 U.S.C. § 77e
    • Rule 10b-517 C.F.R. § 240.10b-5
    • Item 40417 C.F.R. § 229.404
  2. 2

    Arrange and title the parts.

    Put the provisions in the order you teach them. Name each part, and put your course, term, and name on the cover.

  3. 3

    Download the PDF.

    The book has clickable contents, PDF bookmarks, and cross-references that link from one provision to another. Print it, bind it, or post it to the course site.

For students An offline reader they can highlight and annotate. See a sample reader.

§ 4Questions

Short answers.

Is it free? Is it legal?
Both. Statutes and regulations are works of the United States government and are in the public domain (17 U.S.C. § 105). The studio costs nothing and has no accounts. Commercial publishers’ annotations and headnotes are theirs, and none are used here.
How current is the text?
Every supplement prints the release of the U.S. Code and the date of the eCFR it was built from. Today that is U.S. Code release point {{USC_RELEASE}} and the eCFR as of {{ECFR_DATE}}. Open a saved link later and the same selection is rebuilt from the newer text.
Which browser should I use?
Any current browser, on a computer or a phone. It is tested in Chrome and Safari.
Does anything leave my computer?
No. The studio runs in your browser, and your supplement is built there. A share link carries only the outline: the provisions you chose, their order, and the titles and cover text you typed. That outline sits in the part of the link after the #, which browsers do not send to any server.
Can I build a supplement for another course?
Not yet. Federal securities law is what is covered today (see § 3 above). Other bodies of law are planned.
